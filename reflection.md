# CSCI 496 Reflection Essay

## 1. Academic Challenge

One of the biggest challenges I have overcome during my academic development was starting over in a completely new field. I had spent most of my career in retail management, and although I enjoyed leading and teaching people, I eventually realized I did not feel fulfilled. After losing my job, I knew I did not want to fall back into the same cycle. Going back to college was also important to me personally because I had not finished the first time, and that had always been one of my biggest regrets. I had wanted to earn a college degree since I was young, so when I went back to school I wanted to make sure I was choosing a path that actually interested me rather than simply choosing business because it matched my previous experience.

Before deciding on a degree, I met with several deans at Trident Technical College to talk through the programs I was considering. My eventual advisor asked me why I liked computers and helped me think more seriously about my interest in technology and how things work. That conversation led me toward networking and cybersecurity. My first class was Linux Essentials, and it honestly felt like learning a new language. My experience with command-line environments before that was extremely limited. Suddenly I was learning Linux commands, networking terminology, protocols, acronyms, the OSI model, and concepts I had never even heard of before. The challenge was definitely technical, but it was also nontechnical because I had to become comfortable being a beginner again after spending years in a career where I was used to having experience and knowing what I was doing.

At first, I relied heavily on handwritten notes because that had always been how I learned. Writing things down helped me create a quick reference, but I eventually realized it was not an efficient way to learn the amount of technical information I was being introduced to. My advisor, who also taught my networking class, was very good at simplifying difficult concepts and helped me realize that I needed to change the way I studied. I started using practice quizzes, repeated self-testing, and hands-on exercises instead of relying mainly on memorization. Repetition is important for me to retain and internalize concepts, and I found that I learned the material much better when I could work through a concept several times and then actually apply it. I had to restructure not only the way I studied, but also the way I approached learning something unfamiliar.

Over time, the material that initially seemed completely foreign started to make sense. Labs, cybersecurity competitions, and other hands-on experiences helped build my confidence and showed me that I could apply what I was learning instead of just remembering terminology for a test. The more I learned, the more interested I became in cybersecurity. What started as a major academic and career change became something I genuinely enjoyed. Looking back at where I started, I think the biggest thing I gained was learning how to learn in a completely different way. Earning my degree means a great deal to me because it is something I once left unfinished, but it also means more now because I feel like I finally found a field where I belong.

## 2. Teamwork

One of the best examples of teamwork during my academic development came from participating in three cybersecurity competitions in 2024. Going into the first competition, I honestly had no idea what to expect or even where I would be able to contribute. Many of my teammates had more technical experience than I did, so I leaned on skills I already had from years of leadership. The environment was fast-paced and somewhat chaotic, with people working on different virtual machines and trying to figure out which systems they could access and what needed attention. I started keeping track of which systems people were working on, when access was gained, password changes, and other information the team needed to keep organized. I may not have been the strongest technical person in the room, but I could still find a way to help the team function better.

As I participated in more competitions, my role began to change. I was still helping keep the team organized, but I was also becoming more technically involved. By the later competitions, I was working alongside my teammates to bring systems back online and troubleshoot problems instead of mainly documenting what everyone else was doing. At the NCAE cybersecurity competition, our team finished second, and my teammates selected me as the Most Improved Team Member. That meant a great deal to me because they had seen where I started and how much I had grown over the course of the competitions.

I would describe myself as an adaptable teammate. I do not think every person on a team has to contribute in the same way. Sometimes the most valuable thing you can do is recognize what the team needs and step into that role. My years in retail leadership taught me how important it is to involve people, understand what motivates them, and give them ownership instead of trying to do everything yourself. The competitions reminded me that those skills still had value even though I was now applying them in a completely different environment.

The area where I still need to improve is communicating technical problems clearly and confidently. I can often understand what I believe is happening or what the problem may be, but explaining that thought process to someone else can be much harder. I also tend to be quieter now because I understand how much I still have to learn. Going from being the person everyone came to for answers in my previous career to being one of the least experienced people in the room has required humility and patience. I have no problem admitting when I do not know something, but it can still be frustrating. I am learning to be patient with my own development and remind myself that every problem I work through and every new experience is helping me build the technical knowledge and confidence I need.

## 3. Career in Computer Science

The type of career I believe I am most suited for is digital forensics and cyber investigations. I have always been interested in understanding how and why things happen, and cybersecurity has given me a technical way to pursue that interest. I enjoy digging into a problem, following the available evidence, and trying to determine what happened rather than only fixing the immediate issue. As I have progressed through cybersecurity, I have also rediscovered how naturally curious and detail-oriented I am. I like asking questions, looking for patterns, and continuing to work through a problem until the pieces begin to make sense.

Digital forensics appeals to me because it combines technical knowledge with investigation. A forensic investigator can examine a system, follow a trail of data, reconstruct activity, determine where something originated, and preserve evidence that may be important to an investigation. I took a Digital Forensics course at Trident Technical College, and it ended up being one of my favorite courses. I gained hands-on experience with tools such as Autopsy, FTK Imager, and Volatility, along with concepts such as chain of custody and evidence preservation. That course made me even more interested in the field because it showed me how much information can be discovered by carefully examining what was left behind.

My current work in cybersecurity has also helped develop skills that I believe transfer well to digital forensics. RMF work requires attention to detail, documentation, reviewing evidence, understanding security controls, and being able to support conclusions with documented evidence. I appreciate networking, RMF, systems, and security operations, but what excites me most is using those technical foundations to investigate incidents and determine what actually happened.

I am more drawn to this field than other areas of computer science because the investigative side of cybersecurity feels like the best fit for how I think. I respect areas such as software development, network administration, and security operations, but I would rather spend my career following evidence, reconstructing events, and helping determine the cause and impact of an incident. I would also like my work to have a meaningful purpose by helping protect people and organizations while supporting investigations that contribute to public safety or national security.

## 4. Professional Organizations and Development

One professional organization I would like to stay involved with is Charleston Women in Tech. I attended one of their events while I was still trying to establish myself in cybersecurity, and it showed me firsthand how valuable professional networking can be. I met someone there who encouraged me to share my résumé with her organization’s HR department, and that connection eventually helped open the door to my current cybersecurity career. That experience made me realize that professional organizations are not just about adding another membership to a résumé. They can provide mentorship, connections, and exposure to people who are further along in the field. I would also like to become involved with the Information Systems Security Association because it is specifically focused on cybersecurity and would give me opportunities to continue learning from others in the industry.

Over the next five years, I want my professional development to focus on becoming stronger in the work I am already doing while also moving closer to my long-term interest in digital forensics and cyber investigations. I currently hold CompTIA Security+, and one certification I plan to work toward is the CISSP as I gain the experience required for it. I also want to pursue certifications and training focused on digital forensics, but I want to research those carefully and choose credentials that are respected and useful in the type of work I eventually want to do rather than collecting certifications just to have them.

I also plan to continue my formal education. After completing my bachelor's degree, I intend to take some time away from school so I can concentrate on gaining experience and becoming better at the work I am doing now. After that break, my goal is to pursue a master's degree through Georgia Tech. Between graduate education, certifications, hands-on experience, conferences, and involvement in professional organizations, I hope the next five years will help me build both the technical knowledge and professional network I need for the direction I want my cybersecurity career to take.

## 5. Serving the Community

One of the ways I can use the skills I have learned in cybersecurity to serve my community is by helping people better protect themselves from fraud, scams, phishing, identity theft, and other common cyber threats. Cybersecurity can feel overwhelming to people who do not work in technology, and many attacks succeed because people do not know what warning signs to look for or what steps they should take to protect themselves. I believe there is value in taking technical information and explaining it in a way that is practical and easy to understand.

I have already had the opportunity to do some of this through the Cyber Operations Clinic at Trident Technical College. Our team worked with a local small business to identify security risks and explain our findings, and I also helped develop and deliver a cybersecurity awareness workshop for community members age 55 and older. That experience showed me that cybersecurity knowledge can have a direct impact when it is shared with people who may not otherwise have access to that kind of information.

Going forward, I would like to continue helping people understand basic steps they can take to protect themselves, such as recognizing phishing attempts, using multi-factor authentication, creating stronger passwords, protecting personal information, and knowing what to do if they believe an account has been compromised. I am especially interested in helping people recognize fraud before they become victims. I also want to become better at translating technical cybersecurity concepts into language that makes sense to people who do not work in the field, because information is only useful if people can understand how to apply it. As my own knowledge grows, I would like to continue finding ways to share what I learn through community organizations, professional groups, or other educational opportunities.

I think cybersecurity professionals have a responsibility to use their knowledge for more than protecting systems and organizations. I also have a strong desire to serve my country, and I would like to use the skills I am developing in a way that contributes to public safety and national security. Whether that means helping individuals protect themselves from fraud or eventually supporting investigations that protect larger communities, I want the work I do to have a meaningful purpose. If I can use what I have learned to protect people, prevent harm, or help uncover what happened after an incident, then I am using my education in a way that serves both my community and my country.
